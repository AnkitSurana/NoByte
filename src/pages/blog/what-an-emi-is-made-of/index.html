What an EMI is actually made of

A loan payment stays the same every month, but what it is buying changes. Why early payments are almost all interest, and what that means for paying extra.

One payment, two jobs

Every instalment on a normal loan does two things at once: it pays the interest that has built up since the last payment, and whatever is left over reduces the balance you owe. The total is fixed. The split between those two jobs is not.

Interest is charged on what you still owe. At the start you owe almost the whole loan, so the interest portion is large and very little goes to the balance. As the balance falls, so does the interest, and more of the same payment starts clearing principal. This is why the split shifts month by month even though nothing about the payment changes.

What that looks like

Take 20,00,000 borrowed at 9% over 20 years. The payment works out to about 17,995 a month.

  • In the first month, 15,000 of that 17,995 is interest. About 83% of the payment, and roughly 3,000 comes off the balance.
  • Halfway through the term, after ten years of payments, you still owe about 14,20,000. That is 71% of the original loan, after paying for half the term.
  • Over the full 20 years you pay about 43,19,000 in total, of which 23,19,000 is interest. The interest is larger than the amount borrowed.

None of this is a trick. It is what charging interest on an outstanding balance produces, and every lender's schedule works this way.

Why paying extra early does so much

An extra payment goes straight against the balance. That balance is what all future interest is calculated on, so every unit you knock off early stops earning interest for the lender for the entire remaining term.

The same extra payment made in the final years saves very little, because there is not much time left for interest to accrue. This is the practical reason prepayments are worth most at the start, when the balance is high and the remaining term is long.

Where the numbers come from
