What compounding actually does to savings

Compounding looks slow for years and then stops looking slow. Why time matters far more than the compounding frequency, and what a projection can and cannot tell you.

The returns start earning returns

Simple interest pays you on what you put in. Compound interest pays you on what you put in plus everything it has already earned. Each period starts from a slightly larger base than the last, so the growth curve bends upward instead of running in a straight line.

For the first several years the bend is barely visible, which is why compounding feels like a disappointment early on. The effect is real the whole time; it is just small relative to what you are contributing.

Doubling the time does much more than doubling

Put 5,000 a month away at an assumed 12% a year:

  • After 10 years you have contributed 6,00,000 and the balance is about 11,50,000. Growth of roughly 5,50,000.
  • After 20 years you have contributed 12,00,000 and the balance is about 49,46,000. Growth of roughly 37,46,000.

The contributions doubled. The growth went up nearly seven times. The second decade does most of the work, because it is the only decade that gets to compound on top of the first one. Time in the market is doing more here than the size of the monthly amount.

Compounding frequency matters less than people think

Daily compounding sounds meaningfully better than yearly. It is not, at ordinary rates. Take 1,00,000 at 8% for ten years:

  • Compounded yearly: about 2,15,892
  • Compounded quarterly: about 2,20,804
  • Compounded monthly: about 2,21,964
  • Compounded daily: about 2,22,535

The whole spread from yearly to daily is around 3%. Adding a single year to the term is worth more than switching from yearly to daily compounding. When a product advertises its compounding frequency as the headline feature, that is the least important number on the page.

A projection is an assumption, not a forecast

Every figure above depends on a rate that was typed in. Real returns are not a fixed percentage delivered evenly, and a calculator cannot know what markets will do. What these numbers are good for is comparison: what a longer term does, what a higher contribution does, what happens if the rate is two points lower than you hoped. Run the pessimistic version too. That one is more useful than the optimistic one.
