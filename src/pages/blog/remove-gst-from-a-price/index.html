How to take GST out of a price

Working backwards from a tax-inclusive price is not the same as subtracting the rate. Here is the arithmetic, the mistake almost everyone makes, and how to check a figure.

Adding tax is the easy direction

If you know the price before tax, adding it is straightforward. At 18%, a net price of 1,000 becomes 1,180. You multiply by 1.18.

The direction people get wrong is the other one: you have a total of 1,180 that already includes tax, and you need to know how much of it was tax.

The mistake

The instinct is to take 18% off the total. That gives 1,180 minus 212.40, or 967.60. It looks reasonable and it is wrong.

The reason is that the 18% was never charged on 1,180. It was charged on 1,000. Taking 18% of the larger number removes too much, because you are applying the rate to a base that already has the tax folded into it.

The arithmetic that works

Since the gross is the net multiplied by 1.18, you get back by dividing rather than subtracting:

  • Net = gross divided by (1 + rate). Here, 1,180 / 1.18 = 1,000.
  • Tax = gross minus net. Here, 1,180 - 1,000 = 180.

So the tax inside 1,180 is 180, not 212.40. The two methods differ by 32.40 on a single small invoice, and the gap grows with the rate: at 28% the wrong method is off by nearly 8% of the total.

The same formula covers every rate. For 5%, divide by 1.05. For a VAT rate of 20%, divide by 1.2. Nothing about it is specific to one country's tax.

A quick sanity check

Whatever number you land on, test it forwards. Take your net figure, add the rate, and see whether you get the total you started with. 1,000 plus 18% is 1,180, so 1,000 is right. 967.60 plus 18% is 1,141.77, which is not the total on the invoice, so it is wrong.

That check takes a few seconds and catches the error every time, including the one where the rate itself was entered wrongly.
